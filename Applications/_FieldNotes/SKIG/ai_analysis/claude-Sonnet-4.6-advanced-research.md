# Convexity of Conflict: Defense Industry Financial Performance and the Ukraine-Russia War

**The Ukraine-Russia war has generated extraordinary, asymmetric financial returns for defense companies — returns that accelerate with escalation severity and flow to executives through compensation structures with bounded downside.** This report compiles verifiable data across five analytical dimensions to support an academic investigation into whether the relationship between conflict escalation and defense stock returns is convex (non-linear, accelerating), and whether executive compensation structures create asymmetric payoff profiles that reward escalation-driven gains while insulating CEOs from meaningful loss.

Since Russia's full-scale invasion on February 24, 2022, Rheinmetall AG shares have risen approximately **1,576%**, BAE Systems approximately **228%**, and Lockheed Martin approximately **66%** — all outperforming the S&P 500's **58.8%** gain over the same period. Meanwhile, defense CEO total compensation packages range from **$4.9M to $24.1M annually**, with 55–76% tied to equity, and severance provisions guarantee multi-million-dollar exit packages regardless of circumstances.

### Performance metrics driving compensation

**Annual incentive (short-term) metrics by company:**

- **Lockheed Martin:** Sales (20%), Segment Operating Profit (40%), Free Cash Flow (40%), plus strategic/operational measures (30% modifier)
- **RTX:** Adjusted EPS, ROIC, Cash Flow, Corporate Responsibility Scorecard
- **Northrop Grumman:** Cash Flow from Operations (35%), Segment Operating Income Growth (35%), Adjusted Operating Margin (20%), Non-financial (10%)
- **BAE Systems:** Underlying EPS, **Order Intake** (most directly conflict-related), Free Cash Flow, Non-financial (25%)
- **Rheinmetall:** Earnings Before Taxes (EBT), Return on Capital Employed (ROCE), with ±20% modifier

**Long-term incentive metrics:** All five companies use **Relative Total Shareholder Return** as a primary LTI metric (typically 33–50% weighting), alongside ROIC and cumulative free cash flow over 3-year performance cycles. TSR is the metric most directly sensitive to escalation-driven share price appreciation.

**Critical finding: No compensation plan explicitly ties pay to order backlog growth, international defense revenue, or geopolitical conflict intensity.** However, these factors are indirectly captured through revenue, earnings, and TSR metrics. BAE Systems comes closest by using Order Intake as a specific annual bonus KPI. The effect is structural rather than explicit: conflict drives demand → demand drives revenue/earnings/backlog → financial performance drives compensation metrics → stock appreciation drives equity holdings value.

**Clawback provisions** now exist at all five companies (mandatory post-SEC 2023 rules for U.S. firms; malus/clawback provisions for UK/German firms). However, clawbacks apply to restatements and misconduct — not to "windfall" gains from geopolitical events.

### The asymmetry is real but not absolute

**Evidence of some downside risk:** Annual bonuses can decline materially — LMT's Segment Operating Profit component paid **0%** in FY2024 (below threshold), driving overall bonus to 89% of target. PSUs can pay 0–200%, with a TSR cap at 100% if absolute TSR is negative. Stock ownership requirements (LMT CEO: 6× salary ≈ $10.5M minimum) create genuine portfolio exposure to declines. The Boeing precedent demonstrates that in catastrophic scenarios, even defense CEOs can forfeit significant unvested compensation.

**Evidence of substantial downside protection:** Base salary ($1.45M–$1.79M) is guaranteed regardless of performance. RSUs vest on schedule (~$16.9M at LMT) with only stock price exposure. Severance floors are massive: LMT CEO layoff = ~$15.3M cash; CIC = ~$54.6M total. Pension and deferred compensation accumulate independently. **No departing defense CEO has left empty-handed** — even Boeing's fired Muilenburg retained ~$62M. Post-departure "soft landings" (board seats at $250K–$350K each, advisory roles) provide ongoing income.

**The structural verdict:** The "zero downside" characterization is an overstatement, but the **asymmetry is severe**. Stock price appreciation from escalation-driven demand flows directly to CEO equity holdings and TSR-linked incentives, with no mechanism to claw back those gains if the escalation context subsides. The structure functionally rewards being in the right industry at the right geopolitical moment, with meaningful but bounded downside.

## PART 5: Convexity Analysis Framework

### Testing whether escalation produces accelerating returns

The central empirical question is whether defense stock returns exhibit a **convex** (non-linear, accelerating) relationship with conflict escalation severity — i.e., whether a move from severity 40 to 60 produces proportionally larger returns than a move from 20 to 40. The quadratic regression model captures this:

**Returns = β₀ + β₁(Severity) + β₂(Severity²) + ε**

A **positive and statistically significant β₂** indicates convexity: returns accelerate as severity increases. If β₂ ≈ 0, the relationship is approximately linear. If β₂ < 0, it is concave (diminishing returns at higher severity — perhaps because extreme escalation introduces market-wide risk that offsets sector-specific gains).

### Framework 1: Escalation-return correlation

With ~20 event observations per company, statistical power is limited. A Pearson correlation test requires approximately **n = 29** observations to detect a medium effect (r = 0.5) at α = 0.05 with 80% power. Given the dataset size, **Spearman rank correlation** is recommended as a primary test — it is robust to outliers, does not assume normality, and is appropriate for ordinal severity ratings.

Key confounders to control for include: (a) concurrent S&P 500 returns (market-wide movements); (b) pre-existing price trends (momentum); (c) temporal clustering of events (events #4, #5, #6 in Sep–Oct 2022 overlap); and (d) differential media attention across events. Using **abnormal returns** (actual return minus CAPM-expected return) rather than raw returns partially addresses market-wide confounding.

### Framework 2: Model selection for convexity

Compare nested models using:

- **F-test for nested models:** H₀: β₂ = 0 (linear is sufficient) vs. H₁: β₂ ≠ 0 (quadratic is needed). F = [(RSS_linear − RSS_quadratic) / 1] / [RSS_quadratic / (n − 3)].
- **Information criteria:** Lower AIC/BIC for quadratic model supports convexity. BIC penalizes complexity more heavily and is conservative for small samples.
- **Significance thresholds:** Given exploratory analysis with small samples, p < 0.10 for β₂ may be reported alongside standard p < 0.05 thresholds. R² improvement from linear to quadratic should exceed ~5% to be substantively meaningful.

**Robustness checks:** (1) Leave-one-out cross-validation to assess outlier sensitivity (the invasion event at severity 70 / +14–46% return may be highly influential); (2) bootstrapped confidence intervals (10,000 resamples) for β₂; (3) White's test for heteroscedasticity; (4) analysis with and without the invasion event to test whether results are driven by a single observation.

### Framework 3: Event study methodology

Compute abnormal returns using the **market model**:

1. **Estimation window:** [-250, -30] trading days before each event to estimate α̂ and β̂ from regression: R_defense = α + β(R_market) + ε
2. **Event window:** [-5, +5] trading days around the event
3. **Abnormal return:** AR_t = R_defense,t − (α̂ + β̂ · R_market,t)
4. **Cumulative abnormal return:** CAR = Σ AR_t over the event window
5. **Test statistic:** t = CAR / σ̂(CAR), where σ̂ is estimated from the estimation window residuals

Partition events into high-severity (≥60) and low-severity (<60) groups. Test whether mean CAR differs significantly between groups using a two-sample t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

### Framework 4: CEO compensation lag analysis

Test the **1-year lagged relationship** between annual stock returns and next-year CEO total compensation. With only 4–5 annual observations per CEO, individual-level regression is underpowered. A **panel data approach** pooling across all 6 CEOs (approximately 20–24 CEO-year observations) increases power. Use fixed effects to control for company-specific compensation baselines:

**TotalComp_it = α_i + β₁(StockReturn_i,t-1) + β₂(Revenue_it) + ε_it**

Given the dominance of equity in total compensation (55–76%), expect a positive β₁. The more interesting question is whether the elasticity of compensation to stock returns is greater during conflict-escalation years than during pre-conflict years — testable with an interaction term.

### Endogeneity, limitations, and caveats

**Endogeneity concerns** are the most serious methodological challenge. Defense stock prices may move in anticipation of escalation (forward-looking markets), meaning the measured return around an event captures only the *surprise* component. Events that were partially anticipated (e.g., the invasion itself was widely signaled by intelligence disclosures) will show muted event-day returns relative to their severity. Conversely, events that are genuine surprises (Wagner mutiny, Kursk incursion) may show larger returns relative to severity. This creates a **measurement error in the independent variable** (severity captures ex-post importance, not ex-ante surprise) that attenuates correlation estimates.

**Event window selection** significantly affects results. A [-1, +1] day window captures immediate reactions but may miss slower institutional rebalancing. A [-5, +5] day window captures more of the response but introduces noise from confounding events. The 1-week window used here is a reasonable middle ground, but sensitivity analysis across windows (1-day, 3-day, 5-day, 10-day) should be reported.

**Multiple testing corrections** are needed when testing three companies simultaneously. A Bonferroni correction (α = 0.05/3 ≈ 0.017) or Holm-Šidák procedure should be applied. With small samples, this further reduces statistical power.

**Small sample size** (~20 observations) is the binding constraint. Power analysis suggests that only large effects (r > 0.55) can be detected reliably at conventional significance levels. The analysis should be framed as **exploratory and hypothesis-generating** rather than confirmatory. Bootstrap and permutation tests provide more reliable inference than asymptotic t-tests in this setting.

**Omitted variable bias** arises from factors correlated with both severity and returns that are not controlled for: defense budget announcements, earnings releases near event dates, sector rotation dynamics, interest rate changes, and broader geopolitical developments beyond Ukraine. A multivariate regression controlling for VIX changes, interest rate movements, and defense budget news would partially address this.

## Conclusion

This investigation reveals three structural findings that frame the convexity hypothesis. First, the **magnitude of returns is extraordinary and geographically differentiated** — Rheinmetall's 1,576% gain dwarfs LMT's 66%, reflecting that European defense stocks were repriced from structural underinvestment whereas U.S. defense primes were already priced for steady-state demand. This geographic differential itself suggests non-linearity: the *marginal* impact of escalation on previously undervalued stocks may be much larger.

Second, **executive compensation structures create asymmetric payoff profiles by design, not by accident.** With 55–76% of compensation in equity, defense CEOs capture escalation-driven stock appreciation directly through TSR-linked incentives and portfolio gains. The downside is bounded by guaranteed base salaries ($1.5–1.8M), time-vesting RSU tranches (~$13–16M), and severance provisions ranging from ~$15M to ~$55M. No compensation metric explicitly rewards conflict — but the transmission mechanism from escalation → demand → revenue → stock price → CEO wealth is structurally embedded.

Third, the **statistical framework for testing convexity is feasible but underpowered.** The 20-event dataset can detect large effects but not subtle non-linearities. The quadratic regression provides the cleanest convexity test, but results should be treated as hypothesis-generating. The most robust finding is likely to be the **event study result** — that cumulative abnormal returns for defense stocks are significantly positive during the conflict period, with the highest CARs concentrated around the highest-severity events. A future study with higher-frequency data (daily returns around each event) and a larger event set (including non-Ukraine geopolitical events for out-of-sample validation) would substantially strengthen the analysis.

The data assembled here supports a nuanced version of the convexity claim: **defense stock returns are positively associated with escalation severity, the relationship may be non-linear (particularly for European defense stocks undergoing structural repricing), and executive compensation structures ensure that these gains flow asymmetrically to insiders with bounded downside risk.** Whether this constitutes a market failure, a rational pricing of risk, or an ethical concern about the political economy of defense spending is a normative question this data can inform but not resolve.
