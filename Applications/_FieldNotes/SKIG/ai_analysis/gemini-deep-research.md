# Geopolitical Risk and Capital Allocation: A Convexity Analysis of the Defense Industrial Base (2022–2026)

The global security landscape underwent a fundamental transformation starting in early 2022, transitioning from a post-Cold War era defined by "peace dividends" to a period of high-intensity attrition warfare and structural rearmament.1 This report examines the interplay between geopolitical escalation in the Ukraine-Russia conflict and the financial performance of the primary defense contractors serving the Western alliance. By correlating military milestones with equity returns, executive compensation structures, and contractual downside protections, this analysis identifies a distinct convexity in the profit models of the defense industrial base. The period between February 2022 and February 2026 serves as a longitudinal case study in how systemic volatility is internalized and monetized by large-scale defense entities.3

## Strategic Synthesis and Nuanced Conclusions

The defense industrial base has successfully internalized the "forever war" scenario into its capital structure.3 The period from February 2022 to February 2026 proves that the sector does not just survive during conflict; it thrives through a convex relationship between violence and valuation.4

The "zero downside" claim for leadership is supported by the contractually mandated severance floor, which ensures that even a sudden cessation of hostilities or a change in administration would not impair the personal wealth accumulated by executives during the escalation phase.8 Furthermore, the move toward "golden handcuffs" and long-term retention schemes suggests that the industry anticipates high-intensity threat environments to persist well into the 2030s, regardless of the tactical outcome in Ukraine.3

As of February 2026, the record backlogs—$194 billion for Lockheed Martin and $268 billion for RTX—provide revenue visibility that extends beyond the immediate needs of the current conflict.12 This structural backlog acts as a "dam" that will release profits over the next decade, ensuring that the defense sector remains a high-convexity, low-beta pillar of the global financial system. The synthesis of geopolitical risk and capital allocation demonstrates that the defense industry is perhaps the only sector where systemic fragility in the world order translates directly into robust, accelerated financial gains for both the corporation and its executive leadership.
