My Experience of Moving to Germany

My journey to Germany started when I learned that Germany offers a one-year visa that allows people to search for a job. In January 2025, I began researching this opportunity. I carefully reviewed the requirements, identified which documents were needed, and gathered everything necessary by February.

The process required uploading all documents to the consulate’s online portal before the interview. The purpose of this step was to verify that the application package was complete and correct. I uploaded all required documents and received confirmation that everything was in order. I was told that I could proceed to schedule an interview and bring the original documents.

I scheduled the appointment and attended the interview with all originals. However, during the interview, I was unexpectedly asked for additional documents. I explained that I had already submitted everything according to the official list and that my documents had been pre-approved through the portal. The consul responded vaguely, saying that everything looked fine and that they would contact me if needed.

About one and a half months later, I received a rejection stating that my application was denied due to doubts about the authenticity of my documents.

Fortunately, at that time it was still possible to file an appeal. For every document initially required, I provided multiple supporting documents to strengthen my case. After another one and a half months, I finally received confirmation that my visa was approved.

At the end of September 2025, I moved to Germany.

Before arriving, I had already participated in interviews with German companies. However, in most cases, once it became clear that I am Russian, I was told that they would proceed with other candidates. Only in a few cases were there different reasons unrelated to my nationality.

After arriving in Germany, I found accommodation and received a rental contract. With this contract, I registered my address and obtained a registration certificate (Anmeldung), which should allow opening a bank account.

However, when I went to banks, the process was unexpectedly difficult. In several cases, after simply seeing my passport, bank employees refused to open an account without further explanation. After trying three different banks, I was only able to open an account with an online bank. A German bank account was only opened successfully about six months after my arrival — and even then, my first functioning account was actually with a Belgian online bank.

Another requirement for staying in Germany was opening a blocked account, from which a fixed amount is released monthly for living expenses.

Recently, I was informed that I needed to transfer this blocked account from one bank to another. I provided all required documents, including my passport, visa, and registration.

After that, the situation escalated. I started receiving additional requests asking about political affiliations, my status in Germany, and other personal details. I answered all questions and provided all available documents.

Then I was asked to prove that I am not married, allegedly because my name matches someone on a sanctions list.

This requirement is problematic, because while it is possible to prove that someone is married (with a marriage certificate), there is generally no standard document that proves a person is not married, especially across different countries.

Despite this, I was told that unless I provide such proof, my account process will not continue. The communication has been quite strict, and it is unclear what will happen to my funds if I am unable to provide a document that does not realistically exist.
